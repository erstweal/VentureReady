# What You Bury in the Deck Comes Back in Q&A

Most founders rehearse their pitch until they can deliver it in their sleep. Every transition timed, every slide memorized, the closing line polished.

Then the deck ends, someone across the table leans forward, and asks:

**"How did you get to that market size?"**

And the founder, who has rehearsed everything except this, says:

**"We used a top-down estimate from an industry report."**

It's not a disaster. But the next question is sharper, and the one after that is sharper still, and by the end of the session the investor knows more about what the deck left out than what it put in.

The presentation is the part of the meeting you control. Q&A is the first part the investor controls — and it's where a lot of meetings are actually decided.

---

## Q&A Is an Audit of Your Deck

Investors rarely ask random questions. In our experience, the first question after a pitch usually goes straight to the weakest claim in the deck: the market slide you moved through quickly, the traction number with no retention behind it, the competitor you didn't mention.

That's the uncomfortable part. The questions you'll get are mostly predictable, because they come from your own slides. Whatever you buried in the deck is what comes back in Q&A.

Which also means the best way to prepare for Q&A isn't to rehearse clever answers. It's to read your own deck the way the investor will — looking for the claim that won't survive a follow-up.

---

## Six Answers That End It Early

**1. Defending instead of answering.** A hard question isn't an attack, but it's easy to hear it as one. The tell is "That's a great question" followed by a restatement of the pitch. Investors are checking whether you can hear a difficult question and engage with it, because that's what working with you will be like.

**2. Guessing.** Founders make up numbers on the spot because "I don't know" feels fatal. It isn't. A confident wrong number is worse, because it gets checked in diligence. "I don't have that with me — I'll send it tomorrow" is a perfectly good answer, as long as you actually send it tomorrow. The follow-up becomes evidence that you do what you say.

**3. Answering a different question.** Asked about churn, the founder talks about acquisition. It usually happens out of nerves, not evasion, but it reads as avoidance either way. Answer the question that was asked, then add context if it helps.

**4. The four-minute answer.** A typical Q&A runs about fifteen minutes. Every long answer means fewer questions, and an investor who runs out of time doesn't fill the remaining gaps in your favor. Aim for thirty to sixty seconds, then stop and let them ask the follow-up.

**5. Contradicting your own deck.** A revenue figure in Q&A that doesn't match the slide. A launch date that moved. Investors notice, and inconsistency costs more than a weak number, because it raises the question of which version is true.

**6. Co-founders disagreeing in public.** Two founders contradicting each other, or one talking over the other, is the team dynamic on display in real time. Investors read it as a preview of how the company will make decisions. [The team slide makes a claim about who's driving](https://ventureready.ai/blog/what-investors-read-on-your-team-slide) — Q&A is where that claim gets tested.

## How to Prepare for the Questions Your Deck Invites

**Go slide by slide and write the hardest question each one invites.** Every claim generates one. The market slide invites "how did you calculate that?" Traction invites "what does retention look like?" Competition invites "who else is solving this today?" The Ask invites "why this amount, and what does it get you?" If you can't think of a hard question for a slide, ask someone else to.

**Write a short answer to each, and say it out loud.** Two or three sentences, timed. An answer that reads well on paper often runs long when spoken.

**Rehearse the Q&A with someone who isn't a fan.** A mentor, an advisor, a founder who has raised before. Friends and family ask friendly questions. You need someone willing to ask the one you're hoping nobody asks.

**Build an appendix for the questions you know are coming.** Unit economics, cohort data, a fuller competitive breakdown, the cap table, an [IP summary if you're a spinout](https://ventureready.ai/blog/the-ip-questions-that-end-university-spinout-meetings). Pulling up a backup slide in response to a question is one of the strongest moves available in Q&A — it shows you anticipated it.

**Then go back and fix the deck.** If the same hard question keeps coming up in rehearsal, the answer probably belongs in the deck itself. Preparing for Q&A is also one of the best ways to edit your pitch.

---

## Questions Worth Having an Answer For

A few come up often enough that every founder should have a short answer ready:

- **Why now?** What changed that makes this possible or necessary today?
- **Why you?** What do you know about this problem that others don't?
- **How did you get to that market size?** Bottom-up, with the arithmetic.
- **Who else is solving this?** Including the spreadsheet and the status quo. [We've written about why "no competitors" lands badly](https://ventureready.ai/blog/your-pitch-deck-is-an-infomercial).
- **What would you do with half the money?** It tests whether you know which milestones matter most.
- **What's your biggest risk?** "Execution" is not an answer. Name a real risk and what you're doing about it. Founders who can name their own weakest point are usually more convincing than founders who claim not to have one.

---

## What a Good Q&A Feels Like

There's a reliable sign of how a Q&A is going, and it isn't how friendly the room is.

Early questions tend to test whether the claims are true. When the questions shift from *is this true?* to *how would this work?* — how you'd hire, what the second market looks like, how a partnership might be structured — the investor has started picturing the company with their money in it.

A polite room with easy questions is often the warning sign. The meetings that go somewhere usually get harder before they get warmer.
