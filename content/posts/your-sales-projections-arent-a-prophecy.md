# Your Sales Projections Aren't a Prophecy. They're a Method.

Near the end of most decks there's a slide with a chart that climbs from left to right and a single number at the top of the last bar:

**"Year 5 revenue: $48M."**

And somewhere in the meeting, an investor asks the question that number was never built to answer:

**"What has to be true for that to happen?"**

That's the moment a pro forma either holds up or falls apart. Not because the $48M is too high or too low — nobody in the room believes anyone can see five years out. It falls apart when the founder can't explain where the number came from, and the projection turns out to be a prophecy instead of a method.

---

## Why Investors Ask for Numbers They Know Will Be Wrong

Every experienced investor has watched hundreds of projections miss. In our experience, most of them come in below plan, and the gap grows the further out the projection goes. So why ask for them at all?

Because the projections aren't really being read as a forecast. They're being read as evidence of how you think about your business.

A well-built model shows that you know what actually drives your revenue — how customers are found, how long they take to close, what they pay, how long they stay. It shows what the money you're raising is supposed to do. And it gives the investor a way to size the outcome, which matters more than most founders realize, because [the return math behind an early-stage investment](https://ventureready.ai/blog/the-math-behind-the-exit-slide) depends on how big the company can plausibly get and how long it takes to get there.

The number at the top of the last bar is the least important thing on the slide. The assumptions underneath it are what get evaluated.

## The Crystal Ball and the Method

There are two ways to build a revenue projection.

**The crystal ball** starts from the top: the market is $4 billion, we'll capture 2% by year five, so revenue is $80 million. It's fast, it produces an impressive number, and it tells the investor nothing about whether you know how to get there. We've written about why [the 1% slide reads as a red flag](https://ventureready.ai/blog/your-pitch-deck-is-an-infomercial) — the same logic applies here.

**The method** starts from the bottom: the specific activities that produce revenue, and what each one costs and yields.

Here's what that looks like for a simple B2B software company. The round funds two sales hires. Each takes about four months to get up to speed. Once they're at full speed, each closes three new customers a month at $12,000 a year. Those two reps, in their first full year at speed, add about $864,000 in new annual contract value — before accounting for customers who leave.

Every number in that paragraph can be questioned, and that's the point. An investor can push on the ramp time, the close rate, or the price, and you can show what happens to the result. A top-down projection has nothing to push on. A bottom-up one invites the conversation — and the conversation is where credibility gets built.

The drivers differ by business. A sales-led company models reps, ramp, and quota. A marketing-led company models spend, customer acquisition cost, and conversion. A marketplace models supply and demand separately. But in every case, revenue should be the *output* of the model, not the input.

## Six Tells That Make Projections Look Like Fortune-Telling

**1. Growth that starts the month after the money arrives.** Hiring takes time. Recruiting a strong salesperson often takes two or three months, and they need more time to ramp after that. A revenue curve that bends upward the month after closing the round has skipped all of it.

**2. New hires at full productivity on day one.** Related, and very common. Ramp time is one of the first assumptions investors look for, because leaving it out quietly inflates the first year.

**3. Acquisition costs that never rise.** Your first customers are often the easiest to win — warm introductions, early adopters, people who already knew you. Later customers usually cost more. A model where customer acquisition cost stays flat while volume grows tenfold suggests the founder hasn't thought about who customer number 500 is.

**4. No churn.** Customers leave. Even excellent companies lose some each year. A model with no churn line inflates every year that follows.

**5. Margins that improve with no explanation.** Gross margin often does improve with scale — but there should be a reason in the model: volume pricing from a supplier, automation replacing manual work, a shift in product mix. Margins that simply climb from 40% to 80% read as wishful.

**6. Revenue that triples while expenses stay flat.** More customers mean more support, more infrastructure, more people. If the expense lines don't grow with revenue, the profit at the bottom of the model is fiction.

## Your Use of Funds and Your Projections Have to Tell the Same Story

This is the part founders most often miss, and it's where many models quietly contradict the rest of the deck.

The [use of funds on your Ask slide](https://ventureready.ai/blog/your-ask-slide-is-a-test) and the drivers in your revenue model are describing the same plan from two directions. The use of funds says what the money buys. The model says what that spending produces. They have to agree.

If the projections show revenue tripling through a sales team, the use of funds needs to show the hires that make up that team — and the model needs to show them arriving when the money does, not before. If growth comes from paid marketing, the marketing line in the use of funds needs to be large enough to buy the customers the model counts. If the model shows a new product line in year two, something in the use of funds has to build it.

A quick check: pick the three biggest revenue increases in your model and trace each one back to a specific line in your use of funds. If any of them can't be traced, either the model is counting on growth the round doesn't pay for, or the use of funds is buying something the model ignores.

The same goes for runway. The number of months on your Ask slide should match the month in your model where the cash runs out — and the milestones you're promising should arrive comfortably before that month, not on it.

When the two documents agree, an investor reads a coherent plan. When they don't, the inconsistency costs more than any single aggressive number, because it raises the question of which version is real.

## Show Your Assumptions, and What Happens If They're Wrong

The single most useful page you can add is an **assumptions slide** in your appendix. List each key driver — ramp time, close rate, price, churn, customer acquisition cost, gross margin — and next to each one, its source: your own pilot data, a comparable company, an industry benchmark, or an honest "estimate, to be validated."

Then show one **sensitivity check**: what happens if your sales cycle takes twice as long, or your conversion rate comes in at half? Founders worry this looks like doubt. It usually reads as the opposite. Showing the downside before anyone asks tells an investor you've already stress-tested your own plan — and it's very often one of the [first questions in Q&A](https://ventureready.ai/blog/what-you-bury-in-the-deck-comes-back-in-qa) anyway.

---

## If You're Pre-Revenue

Projections without history are expected to be uncertain. What investors look for is an anchor — something real the model starts from. Signed letters of intent, a pilot with measured results, conversion from an early waitlist, a price a customer has actually agreed to. Build the first year from whatever evidence you have, label it clearly, and let the later years follow from the drivers rather than from ambition.

---

## What Goes in the Deck, and What Goes in the Model

The deck needs a summary: three to five years of revenue, gross margin, and burn, plus the handful of drivers that matter most, and the assumptions slide in the appendix. The full spreadsheet belongs in your data room, where an investor who wants to go deeper can change an assumption and watch the result move.

That's ultimately what a credible pro forma offers. Not a prediction to believe, but a model to test.

Nobody in the room expects you to see the future. They expect you to show your work.
