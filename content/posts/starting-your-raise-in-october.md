# If You're Starting Your Raise in October, You're Raising for January

Every fall, a familiar plan takes shape. A founder finishes the deck in October, figures a raise takes eight to ten weeks, and pencils in a close before the end of the year. Clean books, new money in the bank, a fresh start in January.

It almost never works out that way, and the reason has nothing to do with the company. It's the calendar.

If you're starting a raise this month, the most useful thing you can do is assume you're raising for a January or February close — and then use the next eight weeks to make sure that January goes fast.

---

## Count the Weeks You Actually Have

From mid-October to the holidays looks like ten weeks on paper. It isn't.

Thanksgiving falls on November 26 this year, and the whole week around it is soft — people travel, meetings move, decisions wait until "after the holiday." The second half of December is effectively closed. Christmas and New Year's both land on Fridays, which means the stretch from about December 18 through January 4 is two-plus weeks where very little of consequence gets done.

That leaves roughly six working weeks before Thanksgiving and about three after it. And those three December weeks aren't really available either: investors are closing out their own year — portfolio reviews, year-end planning, holiday commitments, family travel. A first meeting in mid-December is possible. A decision usually isn't.

So the realistic window between now and the holiday shutdown is closer to seven productive weeks than ten.

---

## Now Lay a Real Process Over Those Weeks

Here's where the plan breaks.

Organized angel groups typically run a structured sequence: a screening, a presentation to the membership, diligence, terms, and then the paperwork to close. Many meet monthly. From first contact to money in the bank, three to nine months is normal, and [we've written about why that process looks the way it does](https://ventureready.ai/blog/the-angel-investor-in-its-natural-habitat).

Now map that onto the calendar. Miss the screening cutoff for a group's November meeting, and the next opportunity is December — which some groups thin out or skip entirely — and then January. One missed cutoff in October can push your entire process back six weeks without anyone saying no to you.

Individual angels and funds move faster, but they don't escape the calendar. Diligence requests go out, documents get reviewed, references get called — and every one of those steps has a person attached to it who's about to go on vacation.

---

## The Thanksgiving Test

Here's a practical way to know where you stand.

For a realistic chance at a close before the holidays, by Thanksgiving you need three things: a lead investor identified and committed, terms substantially agreed, and diligence materials already in their hands. Not "in conversations." Committed, agreed, delivered.

If you'll have all three by November 26, a December close is achievable. Push hard.

If you won't, you're closing in January or February. That's not a failure, and it's not a judgment on your company. It's arithmetic — and the sooner you accept it, the more useful the next eight weeks become.

---

## The Runway Problem Nobody Mentions

This matters most for one specific founder: the one whose cash runs short in the first quarter.

If your runway ends in February and you're starting your raise now, the calendar math is working against you in a serious way. A raise that realistically closes in February leaves no margin at all — and fundraising from a position of visible urgency is the weakest position there is. Investors can see it, and it changes the conversation.

If that's you, the question isn't how to close faster. It's how to extend the runway — a bridge from existing investors, cutting burn, pulling revenue forward — so you're not negotiating against a clock. Do that math this week, not in December.

---

## What to Do With October and November

If you've accepted that you're raising for January, the next eight weeks aren't wasted. They're the most valuable part of the raise.

**Have first meetings now — and say plainly that they're early.** "We're planning to raise in January, and I'd like you to know the company before then" is one of the best sentences in fundraising. It takes the pressure off the meeting, and it sets up something investors value enormously: the chance to watch you make progress. A founder who says in October what they're going to do and shows up in January having done it is building the strongest evidence there is.

**Get your materials truly ready.** Deck, financial model, cap table, data room. December is a slow month for meetings and a great month for preparation. Founders who walk into January with a complete data room move through diligence in weeks instead of months.

**Pick a milestone you can announce in January.** A signed pilot, a revenue threshold, a key hire. "Since we last spoke" is a powerful way to open a second meeting — and it's only available if something happened in between.

**Get on the January agendas early.** Many angel groups fill their January presenting slots in November and December. If you want to present in January, apply now.

**Send a December update to everyone you've met.** A short, honest year-end note — what you accomplished, what's next, what you're raising and when. It keeps you top of mind through the holidays, and it often re-engages people who passed earlier in the year.

---

## January Is Crowded

One more thing worth knowing: you're not the only founder who's going to figure this out.

Every January, a wave of founders who stalled in Q4 all start their raises at once. Inboxes fill up. Screening slots go fast. Investors who were patient in October are triaging by mid-January.

The founders who move quickly through that crush are the ones who are already known. They had their first meetings in October. They sent an update in December. When they reach out in January, it's a continuation, not an introduction.

That's the real opportunity in a fall raise. Not closing by Christmas — almost nobody does. It's arriving in January as a company investors have already been watching.
