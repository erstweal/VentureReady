# The Thanksgiving Test: Can Your Family Repeat Your Pitch?

Every founder knows the moment. Somewhere between the turkey and the pie, a relative leans across the table and asks:

**"So what does your company actually do?"**

And the founder, who has given this answer a hundred times, says something like:

**"We're an AI-powered platform that leverages proprietary data to optimize workflows across the mid-market healthcare ecosystem."**

Your aunt nods politely, passes the potatoes, and asks your cousin how his fantasy football team is doing.

It's easy to laugh this off. She isn't your customer, and she certainly isn't your investor. But that moment at the table is one of the most useful pitch tests you'll get all year, and it's free.

---

## The Sentence That Matters Is the One Someone Else Repeats

Here's why the dinner table matters more than it seems.

When an investor likes your company, the next thing that happens usually doesn't involve you. They go back to their partners, their committee, or their angel group, and they describe your company in their own words. You aren't in that room. [Your executive summary may not be either](https://ventureready.ai/blog/your-executive-summary-gets-read-without-you). What's in the room is whatever version of your company the investor can repeat from memory.

If your description only works when you deliver it, with your slides behind you and your enthusiasm carrying it, it falls apart the moment someone else has to say it. And if the investor can't explain what you do in a sentence, it gets much harder for them to argue for you.

So the test isn't whether your aunt understands your technology. It's whether she could repeat what you do to someone else.

If your aunt can't repeat it, neither can an investor's partner.

---

## How to Run the Test This Thursday

It takes about thirty seconds, and you'll probably get the chance whether you want it or not.

**Say it in one sentence.** Not the elevator pitch, not the backstory. One sentence.

**Ask them to say it back in their own words.** This is the part that matters, and the part most founders skip. Don't ask "Does that make sense?" Everyone says yes. Ask, "How would you describe it to your neighbor?"

**Listen to what they get wrong.** The mistakes tell you exactly where the sentence is failing. If they get the customer wrong, your customer isn't specific enough. If they describe the technology but not what it does for anyone, you've led with the how. If they shrug and say "something with computers," you've used too many words that mean nothing outside your industry.

Then try again with whoever is sitting across from you at dessert.

---

## A Sentence That Survives Being Repeated

Most strong one-sentence descriptions share a structure:

**We help [a specific customer] [get a specific outcome] by [how you do it], unlike [what they do today].**

Here's the healthcare platform from the opening, rewritten that way:

*"We help independent pharmacies cut prescription wait times in half by automating insurance approvals, which most of them still handle by phone and fax."*

Your aunt can repeat that. So can an investor's partner. It names who the customer is, what changes for them, how, and what they're doing now, and every word is something a person outside the industry can picture.

Notice what it leaves out: the AI, the data, the platform, the ecosystem. Those may all be true. They just aren't what someone remembers.

---

## Five Ways the Sentence Fails

**1. The jargon stack.** "AI-powered," "platform," "leverage," "optimize," "ecosystem." Each one sounds substantial, and together they make a sentence nobody can repeat, because none of them points at anything a listener can picture.

**2. A category instead of a customer.** "For small businesses" or "for the healthcare space" describes a market, not a buyer. "For independent pharmacies" is someone your aunt can picture walking into.

**3. The how before the what.** Founders often lead with the technology because it's what they spend their days on. Listeners need to know what changes for the customer first. The how is the reason you'll win, not the reason anyone should care.

**4. Trying to say everything.** Three customer types, four features, and a vision, all in one breath. A sentence that covers everything is remembered as nothing. Pick the first customer and the main outcome, and let the rest come in the conversation that follows.

**5. The mission instead of the business.** "We're democratizing access to healthcare" tells a listener what you believe, not what you sell. The mission has a place in your pitch. It just isn't the first sentence.

---

## Where the Sentence Goes After Thursday

Once you have a sentence that survives the dinner table, use it everywhere, word for word.

It belongs at the top of your executive summary, in the first line of every email you send an investor, near the start of your pitch, and in how you introduce yourself at events. Using the same sentence everywhere isn't repetitive. It's what makes it the version people remember and pass along.

It also isn't the same thing as your hook. [We've written about Slide 2 as The Hook](https://ventureready.ai/blog/we-made-a-kpop-ad-for-a-pitch-deck-tool_1), the provocation that makes an investor lean forward. The hook earns attention. The one sentence makes sure that, once you have their attention, they know exactly what you do. Strong decks usually need both, and the sentence comes first, because a provocation about a company nobody understands doesn't go anywhere.

---

This Thursday, when someone asks what your company actually does, take the question seriously. Give them one sentence, ask them to say it back, and listen.

If they can repeat it between the turkey and the pie, you're closer to ready for a room full of investors than you might think.
